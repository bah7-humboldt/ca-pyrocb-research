What a pyroCb looks like on radar

Weather radar can't see smoke: the particles are far too small. It does see the ash, embers and burnt debris that a strong plume lofts, and the rain and ice that form inside a pyroCb. Dual-polarization radar (every NEXRAD site since 2013) can tell the two apart, so a radar near the fire shows how deep the plume really goes and whether it has turned into a thunderstorm.

What the radar sees

A dual-polarization radar sends pulses in two planes, horizontal and vertical, and compares what comes back. Two comparisons sort plume from cloud:

  • Correlation coefficient (CC): how alike the two returns are from pulse to pulse, 0 to 1. Rain, snow and ice give values close to 1; anything ragged, tumbling and mixed in size gives low values.
  • Differential reflectivity (ZDR): whether targets look wider than they are tall (positive, in dB) or taller than wide (negative). Raindrops flatten as they fall, so rain is mildly positive; flat flakes of ash drifting broadside are strongly positive.

Inside a pyroconvective plume

Below cloud base the column is debris: low CC, strongly positive ZDR. Above the condensation level, as cloud water and then ice take over, CC rises and ZDR falls with height; in a well-observed US pyroCb that change, which also appeared toward the edges of the deep cloud, went with lightning (Lang et al. 2014, as summarized by McCarthy et al. 2018). A column of low-CC debris capped by high-CC ice, rooted on the fire, is the pyroCb signature this page looks for.

How this study uses it

  • Debris: CC below 0.80 with at least 5 dBZ, Kumjian's boundary for non-weather echoes. That is generous next to Melnikov's 0.4 and also admits insects and birds, so a column only counts when its lowest echo sits on the past hour's GOES fire pixels and it climbs without big gaps; clear-air insect layers neither start on the fire nor stand 10 km tall.
  • Ice: CC 0.95 or more above 6 km, above the melting layer where CC drops below 0.90.
  • Tested. Re-running every radar volume with stricter debris rules barely changed the result. With CC below 0.60 the 10.8 km test caught 9 of 10 pyroCb days (one slipped from 10.80 to 10.78 km) and the same no-plume day; with each fire held out it scored the same as 0.80. Adding ZDR of +1 dB or more changed nothing: the plume debris already had positive ZDR. CC below 0.40, Melnikov's typical plume value, was too strict for NEXRAD's wide beams: it broke the columns at their base and lost two pyroCb days (Carr 26 July 2018, Dixie 9 August 2021). The insects and birds that the 0.80 rule lets in aren't what makes columns tall.

More than plume height

  • Where the fire is. The strongest low-level echoes tend to sit over the head and flanks of a running fire. Lareau et al. (2022) turned this into fire perimeters from NEXRAD every ~10 minutes, through smoke and cloud, for the Camp Fire and for the Bear run of 8 September 2020 (the North Complex contrast case below), and found long-range spotting driving the fastest spread.
  • Turbulence and rotation. Doppler spectrum width maps turbulence inside the plume, and velocity shows convergence and rotating columns (McCarthy et al. 2018, with a mobile X-band radar a few kilometres from Australian fires).

How well it works

For every fire day with a usable radar, the study traced the tallest echo column rooted on the fire: starting from debris at the lowest beam over the fire and climbing through echoes with no big vertical gaps. A column of 10.8 km or more was the clearest single sign of a pyroCb in the whole study.

Downdrafts, outflow and rotation

The plume's radial velocity (motion toward or away from the radar) can also show the winds a pyroCb makes. Every pyroCb and pyroCu day, plus each fire's biggest day with no deep plume cloud, was searched every 20 minutes from 13:00 to 21:00 PDT within 15 km of the fire, for three signatures:

  • Rotation: an inbound-outbound velocity couplet across about 3 km of arc (rotational velocity Vrot, half the difference). NWS guidance treats 10 m/s as weak, 15 as moderate and 20 as strong rotation at short range (Brown & Wood 2007).
  • Low-level outflow (downburst): divergence along the beam within 2 km of the ground, at least 10 m/s across 4 km, or 0.0025 s−1 (Wilson et al. 1984 and Roberts & Wilson 1989, as used by Gibson & Carlin 2025). The area-integrated version adds up the divergent area.
  • Mid-level convergence: convergence 3–7 km above the ground, the inflow that often comes before a downburst reaches the surface. Gibson & Carlin (2025) found it at 0.0025 s−1 or more in 83% of 53 US downbursts.

No dealiasing: each velocity difference is folded back into ± the Nyquist velocity (24–30 m/s on these scans), which works while the true difference is smaller. A signature must hold across a 3 × 3 patch of gates with at least 5 dBZ of echo, and the beam must clear the ground by 300 m (beams below the terrain see only clutter).

  • Low-level divergence is everywhere: no-plume days reached the downburst threshold as often as pyroCb days, from wind over terrain and noisy plume echo. Only six pyroCb days had a radar within 100 km whose beam cleared the ground, too few to say more.

Firefighter hazards: which factor forecasts them?

On every pyroCb and pyroCu day with a radar within 100 km of the fire (the range Gibson & Carlin 2025 used: 25 days, 6 pyroCb and 19 pyroCu), the study asked two questions: did the downdraft precursors appear, and which of the forecast factors (pyroconvective CAPE, downdraft CAPE, free-convection height zfc) goes with stronger hazard signatures?

Polarimetric downburst precursors

Every scan (about 5 minutes apart) for 90 minutes either side of the day's tallest plume was searched. The method and thresholds are Gibson & Carlin's (2025): the 95th-percentile reflectivity and KDP in height bins, and the median ZDR and 10th-percentile ρhv in the reflectivity core. It was adapted in two ways. Only precipitation echo counts (at least 30 dBZ, ρhv ≥ 0.90, ZDR −2 to 8 dB, at least 10 gates per bin), so plume debris can't fake a signature. What it means for forecasting crew safety

  1. Will there be a pyroCb? Fire power ÷ PFT, with PCAPE, decides that (see Factors).
  2. A pyroCb brings a precipitation core. All six did, and core days carried about twice the outflow and inflow.
  3. How strong will the downdrafts and outflow be? DCAPE is the one factor that tracks it: on pyroCb and pyroCu days with DCAPE above about 1,235 J/kg, outflow reached the downburst threshold on 10 of 13 days, against 5 of 12 below it. It also tracks mid-level convergence (a downburst precursor) and rotation.
  4. PCAPE and a low zfc go with stronger, heavier precipitation cores (descending KDP cores, higher reflectivity), the loading that drives a downburst, but not with the outflow measured near the ground.

Limits. A small sample (25 days, 6 pyroCb) and many tests: one in twenty correlations could reach p 0.05 by chance, so treat DCAPE as a strong lead to verify, not a validated rule. Most fires are 50–100 km from their radar, where the lowest beam is hundreds of metres to a kilometre or more above the ground, so shallow outflows and small vortices are missed. There is no storm tracking: the search covers 15 km around the fire. KDP is approximate. DCAPE assumes a saturated downdraft, which a dry, high-based pyroCb may not have.

Why the 95th percentile (and the median, and the 10th)?

Each height bin over the fire holds many radar gates, so each scan needs one number per bin. The percentiles are Gibson & Carlin's (2025), kept unchanged so the study's thresholds (50 dBZ for a descending reflectivity core, 1 °/km for a KDP core) mean what they mean in that paper. Each one is chosen for the question the signature asks.

StatisticQuestionWhy this percentile
95th-percentile Z and KDP (95Z, 95KDP)How intense is the core in this layer? The maximum is a single gate, and a single gate can be clutter, a sidelobe or a hail spike; KDP, estimated from noisy phase, is worse. The median describes the whole echo, mostly light precipitation, and hides the small heavy core that drives a downburst. The 95th percentile is the value only the top 5% of gates exceed: the core's strength, with outliers discarded ("to discard any outlier values", Gibson & Carlin 2025).
Median ZDR in the core (50ZDR)What is the typical core particle: rain, or ice and hail? The core is the gates at or above the 95th-percentile reflectivity. The question is about the typical particle there, so the middle value answers it. Low ZDR under the freezing level points to melting ice and graupel rather than big raindrops.
10th-percentile ρhv in the core (10ρhv)Is there melting hail or mixed phase? That signature is a drop in ρhv, so it lives in the low tail. The 10th percentile looks there while still ignoring the single lowest gates.

Caveat for this study. Gibson & Carlin isolate a whole tracked storm cell, so each bin holds many gates. Here a bin counts once it has 10 precipitation gates within 15 km of the fire. With 10–20 gates the 95th percentile sits close to the maximum, and the "core" may be only one or two gates, which likely adds noise to the core ZDR and ρhv values (one reason those signatures don't separate the classes). The descending KDP result is steadier but shares the caveat. The downdraft and outflow measures (above) use patch medians against a fixed threshold, not the paper's 90th-percentile divergence.

What a ZDR trough and a ρhv drop are

Both are signs of large ice (hail and graupel) falling through the freezing level inside a descending precipitation core, the core that is about to become a downburst. Gibson & Carlin (2025) found the pair in 47 of 53 U.S. downbursts, typically about 10 minutes before the outflow reached the ground. Carlin & Ryzhkov's (2025) model shows the trough deepening as the downdraft strengthens.

ZDR trough

Differential reflectivity (ZDR) compares the horizontal and vertical returns: big raindrops flatten as they fall and give clearly positive values (+1 to +4 dB); near-spherical or tumbling targets give values near 0. Hailstones and graupel are lumpy, tumble as they fall and, while dry, return weakly (ice has a lower dielectric constant than water), so they pull ZDR toward 0 dB. Under the freezing level a heavy core would normally be rain with high ZDR. A trough is a pocket of low ZDR there: ice that has fallen below 0 °C without yet melting, which a strong downdraft makes possible by bringing it down fast.

ρhv drop

The correlation coefficient (ρhv) measures how alike the horizontal and vertical returns are from pulse to pulse. Pure rain gives above 0.98. As ice melts, the gate holds a mix of shapes, sizes, fall behaviours and part-water, part-ice particles, and large wet hail can scatter resonantly; all of that decorrelates the two returns. A drop below about 0.96 in the same place as the ZDR trough confirms melting ice rather than, for example, small drops.

How they are calculated here

  1. Precipitation gates only. Each scan's gates within 15 km of the fire are kept only if Z ≥ 30 dBZ, ρhv ≥ 0.90 and ZDR between −2 and 8 dB, so plume debris (low ρhv, very high ZDR) can't fake a signature.
  2. Height bins above ground: every 0.5 km to 3 km, every 1 km to 8 km, every 2 km to 16 km (Gibson & Carlin's bins). A bin counts once it has 10 precipitation gates.
  3. The core. In each bin and scan, the gates at or above that bin's 95th-percentile reflectivity (why the 95th).
  4. Core statistics. 50ZDR: the median ZDR of the core gates. 10ρhv: the 10th percentile of their ρhv.
  5. Freezing level. The HRRR 0 °C height above ground at the fire, the median over the day's analyses (3–4 km on these days).
  6. Flags. A day has a ZDR trough if any bin lying wholly below the freezing level, in any scan of the three-hour window, has core 50ZDR below 1 dB. It has a ρhv drop if any of those trough bins also has 10ρhv below 0.96. The thresholds are Gibson & Carlin's.

Why they found almost every day here. Gibson & Carlin required a trough that holds together in time and height on a time–height plot, judged by eye. This study's flag is looser: one qualifying bin in one scan is enough, and with only 10–20 gates per bin the core can be one or two gates. Also, under these high freezing levels the core really is mostly graupel and melting ice, so low ZDR is the normal state, not an exception. And because gates below ρhv 0.90 are removed as possible debris, a drop can only show between 0.90 and 0.96. The result: 14 of 16 core days had a trough and 11 had a drop, so neither separates pyroCb from pyroCu days. On the time–height pictures they are the light cells under the dashed 0 °C line in the ZDR panel and the dark cells in the ρhv panel (how to read those pictures).

Limits

  • Terrain. Radars looking across the Sierra Nevada see ground clutter that reads as a tall column. In this study the Hanford radar (KHNX: Creek, Ferguson) and Sacramento radar (KDAX: Caldor, Mosquito) were unusable for that reason.
  • Range. At 100 km or more the beam is a kilometre or two wide and starts well above the ground, so plume tops blur upward and the base is missed (KBBX for the Ranch Fire).
  • Ordinary storms. Ice from a thunderstorm drifting over the fire looks like an ice cap on the plume; those days are left out of the scoring, as they are for the satellite test. Even a rain core rising above a fire isn't proof: in one Australian case the radar saw convective rain over the plume, but the thunderstorm only matured more than 50 km downwind, amid other storms (McCarthy et al. 2018).
  • Clear-air echo. Insects and birds fill the lowest kilometre or two near the radar on warm afternoons with weak, low-CC echo that can look like debris. The rooted-column test keeps it out by starting on the fire and requiring a tall, continuous column.
  • Plume drift. At long range the lowest beam is high above the ground and sees debris that has already drifted downwind, so echoes sit off the true fire edge (Lareau et al. 2022).
  • After the fact. Radar confirms a pyroCb as it happens. It doesn't forecast one; see Forecasting.
