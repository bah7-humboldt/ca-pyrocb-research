Shared conventions

The site's charts: dots, colours, cuts and axes

Colours

Every dot or bar is one fire day (06:00 to 06:00 local time), coloured by what its plume did: orange pyroCb, blue pyroCu, green ordinary thunderstorms over the fire, grey no deep plume cloud. The class comes from GOES cloud-top temperature (next panel). PyroCb dots are drawn larger and on top so the few of them are never hidden.

Strip charts

One column per plume class, each day jittered sideways at random (the sideways position means nothing). A factor is useful when the orange column sits apart from the others.

Scatter plots

Two factors against each other. A diagonal labelled "0.25" or "1:1" marks a fixed ratio between the axes, for example fire power equal to a quarter of the PFT.

Dashed lines

The best cut: the threshold that best separates pyroCb days from the rest, chosen on all fires (balanced accuracy, below). The score quoted with it is from the leave-one-fire-out test, which is the honest one.

Log axes

Fire power, PFT and their ratio span three orders of magnitude, so they are drawn on log scales: equal distances are equal ratios. A PFT with no solution (air too dry or stable for any pyroCb) is drawn at the top of a log axis or marked ×.

Interaction

Hover a mark for its numbers; click a day to open it on Explore days.

Scores

Balanced accuracy = the average of the share of pyroCb days caught and the share of other days correctly left alone (0.5 = chance, 1 = perfect), so the many non-pyroCb days can't flatter a rule [B10]. Leave-one-fire-out: the cut is chosen on all fires but one, tried on the one left out, and repeated for every fire, so a rule that only fits one fire scores badly. Ordinary-storm days are left out of every score: the satellite can't tell whether a pyroCb hid among them.

Rank correlations

Where relationships are tested (Firefighter safety), Spearman's ρ is used: it compares the order of the days, not their values, so one extreme day can't drive it [SP04].

Sources
[B10] · [SP04] · method: Methods & sources

The plume classes

What it is

The label every day carries: pyroCb, pyroCu, ordinary storms over the fire, or no deep plume cloud.

How it's made

GOES-West band 13 (10.3 µm "clean" infrared) brightness temperature every 10 minutes, 11:00–23:00 PDT [SC17]. A cloud counts as rooted on the fire when its connected cold area reaches within 20 km of a GOES fire pixel from the past hour, after a parallax correction. PyroCb: a rooted top colder than −40 °C in at least two scans with no other cloud that cold within 100 km. PyroCu: colder than −20 °C but not a pyroCb. Every automatic call was then reviewed by eye on the satellite panels.

Why these limits

Below about −40 °C cloud water freezes almost completely, so a top that cold is glaciated, thunderstorm-depth cloud. This is the same infrared approach used to inventory pyroCb from geostationary satellites [P17; F10].

Limits

An anvil from a nearby storm can drift over the fire; cloud shields hide what is underneath (hence the ordinary-storms class); a brief pyroCb between scans or one under 2 km² of cold cloud can be missed.

Sources
[SC17] · [P17] · [F10] · GOES-16/17/18 ABI L1b and FDC via NOAA Open Data on AWS · full rules: Methods

Two roads to a pyroCb Overview

What it shows

The two ways the study's fires reached a pyroCb: a modest fire under very favourable air (low PFT), or a very powerful fire under marginal air (high PFT). Both cross the same line, fire power against the threshold.

Why it matters

It is the reason neither fire power alone nor the atmosphere alone separates pyroCb days, and why the ratio does. Tory & Kepert (2021) frame pyroCb potential exactly this way: a property of the air (PFT) that a given fire either exceeds or not.

PFT calculator Factors

What it shows

The pyroCb firepower threshold from its three ingredients, and where a day with your fire power would sit among the studied days.

How it's made

The simplified form PFT ≈ 0.3 · zfc² · UML · Δθfc (GW, z in km). The study itself uses the full form with air density and an entrainment-weighted mixed layer from HRRR soundings [TK21], which descends from plume-rise theory [MTT56].

How to read it

zfc enters squared, so the height the plume must reach dominates: halving it quarters the threshold. The tiers match the study: FP/PFT around 0.25 and above is where its pyroCb days fell.

[TK21] · [MTT56] · in depth: PFT

Fires

Where it burned Fires

What it shows

Left: the fire (star) among all the study fires. Right: its final perimeter, each study day's hot front coloured by plume class, the radar used, towns and county lines.

How it's made

Perimeters from MTBS (WFIGS for the 2025–26 test fires) [E07; WFIGS]. The hot front is the 0.1° cell with the most fire radiative power in that day's afternoon (12–17 PDT) VIIRS/MODIS detections: the point where the HRRR soundings and PFT are taken. Boundaries and towns from Natural Earth [NE]; radar sites from the NEXRAD network [CA93].

How to read it

Hot fronts show how far the fire moved between pyroCb days, and how far it sat from its radar (beyond about 100 km the lowest beam misses the low plume).

Sources
[E07] · [WFIGS] · [NE] · [CA93]

Growth each fire day Fires

What it shows

Acres burned each fire day (06:00–06:00 local), coloured by plume class.

How it's made

The toolkit's satellite growth set. Each fire's MTBS event is matched to its Fire Program Analysis fire-occurrence records for the start date [SH22]. VIIRS 375 m and MODIS 1 km active-fire detections (NASA FIRMS) inside the final perimeter date the burned area: the perimeter is gridded at 100 m and each cell takes the earliest fire day among the detections near it (nearest-detection interpolation, after Parks 2014), so the daily acres add up to the perimeter's area [S14; G16; D09; P14]. The 2025–26 test fires (WFIGS perimeters) count each 375 m VIIRS cell on the first day it burns, with GOES-19 filling gaps from 2025. A study day is any fire day with 1,000 acres or more of growth or 3,000 MW or more of total detected fire radiative power.

How to read it

The biggest bars are often grey: wind-driven runs without deep plume cloud. PyroCb days are usually large but not the largest.

Limits

Polar orbiters pass about twice a day each, so growth late in the day can land on the next day; cloud and thick smoke hide detections. Validated against the incident reports (IMSR) at rank correlation about 0.6 same day, 0.75 allowing one day's lag.

Sources
[S14] · [G16] · [D09] · [P14] · [E07] · [SH22]

Fire power against the threshold Fires

What it shows

Each day's peak-hour fire power (orange) and the day's lowest PFT (grey), on one log axis in GW. × marks days with no PFT solution.

How it's made

Fire power: GOES fire radiative power summed over the fire's pixels, hourly, × 3.9 to convert radiated to convective heat [S20; W05; TK21]. PFT: from HRRR analyses at the hot front, the lowest of 12–22 PDT [D22].

How to read it

A pyroCb needs the orange line to come close to the grey one: in this study, to about a quarter of it or more. Watch for the grey line dropping (favourable air arriving) while the fire is still burning hard.

Limits

The two lines are each the day's extreme and may come from different hours; the ratio panel below matches them hour by hour. GOES FRP undercounts under thick smoke and saturates on the most intense hours.

Sources
[S20] · [W05] · [TK21] · [D22] · in depth: fire power, PFT

Fuel conditioning Fires

What it shows

How the fuels dried into the study days: the fire's Predictive Services Area (PSA) Energy Release Component and 1000-h fuel moisture against that PSA's normal range and its 90th/97th (10th/3rd) percentile lines, gridMET 1000-h moisture at the fire, and NFDRS live fuel moisture. Study days are shaded orange (pyroCb) or blue (pyroCu).

How it's made

NFDRS fuel model Y, averaged over the PSA's RAWS, from 75 days before the first study day [B84; CD85; J24]. The RAWS observations come from FEMS [Z03]; percentiles are on each PSA's full-year record, March 2005 to 2025, the same basis as the ONCC/OSCC fire danger charts. Live fuel moisture is the NFDRS growing-season-index model [J05]. gridMET from [A13].

How to read it

A pyroCb needs fuel dry enough to burn completely and fast: every study pyroCb day had PSA 1000-h at or below about 10.8% and ERC at or above about 54 (the moisture baseline). It is a precondition: many dry days made no pyroCb.

Limits

A PSA average blends stations that may be far from the fire; gridMET is the at-fire check.

Sources
[B84] · [CD85] · [J24] · [J05] · [A13] · [Z03] · FEMS

Surface weather: RAWS against ERA5 Fires

What it shows

Temperature, relative humidity and wind at the nearest reporting RAWS, against ERA5 at that RAWS and at the fire's hot front, through the study days.

How it's made

RAWS hourly observations from FEMS [Z03]; ERA5 hourly reanalysis from the toolkit's archive [H20]. The bias and mean absolute error of ERA5 at the RAWS are printed on the figure.

How to read it

It shows how far the gridded weather used elsewhere (ERA5 moisture, HRRR soundings) can be trusted at this fire, and what the fire's own surface weather was. Expect ERA5 winds to run low: RAWS wind is a 10-minute mean at 20 ft on exposed sites, ERA5 an hourly 10 m average over a 31 km cell.

Limits

The nearest RAWS can be tens of kilometres away and in different terrain; on smoky days RAWS temperatures can read low under the smoke.

Sources
[Z03] · [H20] · FEMS

Weather patterns

Regimes and what the plume did Weather patterns

What it shows

How often each 500 hPa regime (A–F) occurred on pyroCb, pyroCu and other days, as a lift (how much more often than on all studied days), with the median 700 hPa moisture at the fire.

How it's made

The regimes are the companion AI ensemble viewer's: k-means clusters of daily 500 hPa height anomalies over the northeast Pacific and western North America, fire-season days 2001–2025, from the NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 [K96]. Each study day takes its regime's label. Moisture is ERA5 700 hPa specific humidity at the hot front, the moister of 11 a.m. and 5 p.m. [H20].

How to read it

A lift of 2 means the regime shows up twice as often on that class of day as on all days. Regimes are forecastable days ahead, which is their value: a heads-up before a fire's own power is known.

Limits

Regimes are coarse (six patterns for a whole season); the same regime covers very different local weather.

Sources
[K96] · [H20] · AI ensemble research viewer

Regime against mid-level moisture Weather patterns

What it shows

Every fire day placed by its regime (columns, jittered) and its 700 hPa moisture (height).

How to read it

PyroCb days need both a favourable regime (A, C or D) and moist mid-levels (about 3.7 g/kg or more at 700 hPa, roughly 10,000 ft). Moisture there lowers the condensation level the plume has to reach and adds latent heat once it does, the effect monsoon surges bring under a hot ridge [TTK18; P05].

Sources
[K96] · [H20] · [TTK18] · [P05] · in depth: 700 hPa moisture

Rules from regime and moisture Weather patterns

What it shows

How to read it

Composite 500 hPa maps Weather patterns

What it shows

The average 500 hPa height anomaly on pyroCb days, on pyroCu days, and on big-growth days (20,000 acres or more) without deep plume cloud.

How it's made

Each day's NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 500 hPa height minus that calendar day's 2001–2025 normal, averaged over the class [K96]. Red = higher than normal (ridge), blue = lower (trough).

How to read it

The pyroCb composite is a ridge over British Columbia with a trough offshore, which steers mid-level moisture north over a hot interior. The big no-plume runs are the offshore pattern: a coastal ridge with an inland trough, dry and windy. The same ingredients are documented in the regional critical-fire-weather literature [S64; W11].

Limits

An average can look like no single day; a few strong days dominate small classes.

Sources
[K96] · [S64] · [W11]

Documented Northern California patterns Weather patterns

What it shows

Fire days flagged with the critical fire-weather patterns from the regional literature, counted by plume class: Northern California dry thunderstorms, northeast (offshore) foehn, southwest foehn, the ridge-breakdown stages, and a persisting ridge.

How it's made

The companion viewer's objective event rules: dry thunderstorms = at least 500 lightning strikes in the ONCC PSAs; NE foehn = the San Francisco–Winnemucca pressure gradient past a cutoff; SW foehn = Reno 700 hPa southwest wind of 25 kt or more with a dry slot and few strikes; ridge breakdown stages from the regime sequence (last ridge day before a trough day, then the first trough day), after the Desert Research Institute's CEFA critical-fire thresholds [CEFA], the NWCG field guide [PMS437], the NWS critical patterns compendium [NWS99] and the classic synoptic types [S64].

How to read it

Most pyroCb days came while a ridge persisted. Offshore (NE foehn) days drove some of the largest runs but almost never a pyroCb: the air is too dry for one, and the strong wind bends the plume over. Megafire growth responds most strongly to extreme wind [PM21].

[CEFA] · [PMS437] · [NWS99] · [S64] · [W11] · [PM21] · lightning: Vaisala NLDN (counts only)

What it shows

A hindcast of a morning pyroCb alert. Watch: the 12Z (05 PDT) HRRR afternoon forecast (f09–f15) at the fire front known that morning gives a PFT of 360 GW or less. Radar

Radar plume column by class Radar

What it shows

The tallest echo column rooted on the fire each day (the second-highest scan, to drop one-off spikes), by plume class, with the 10.8 km cut.

How it's made

NEXRAD Level II [CA93]: starting from echo at the lowest beam over the fire, climbing through echoes with no large vertical gaps. Debris is low-ρhv echo; ice on top is high-ρhv [M08; K13; P22; L22]. Radars whose beams hit Sierra terrain clutter on ordinary days (KHNX, KDAX, KBBX for Ranch) are left out.

How to read it

A 10.8 km column was the clearest single sign of a pyroCb in the study. It is an observation, not a forecast.

Sources
[CA93] · [M08] · [K13] · [P22] · [L22] · in depth: radar plume top

Radar pictures of each fire Radar

What it shows

One NEXRAD volume per case, usually the scan with the day's tallest fire-rooted column, in four panels, plus a time–height chart of the column through the afternoon.

The panels

a Highest reflectivity in each column through all tilts, with the final perimeter and the past hour's GOES fire pixels. b Echo top, the highest gate of 5 dBZ or more (km above sea level). c, d Reflectivity and correlation coefficient (CC, ρhv) in a vertical slice along the beam through the fire, every tilt.

How to read it

In the slices, plume ash and debris read CC below about 0.8 and rain or ice above about 0.95. A pyroCb shows a low-CC column rising from the fire capped by high-CC ice; a pyroCu stops lower; a wind-driven run lies flat downwind. Beam heights use the standard 4/3-earth model, so beams rise with range.

Sources
[CA93] · [K13] · [M08] · [P22] · [RZ19] · decoding: MetPy [M]

Downdrafts, outflow and rotation Radar

What it shows

How often three Doppler-velocity hazard signatures appeared over the fire, by class, and how well each separates pyroCb days.

How it's made

Radial velocity every 20 minutes, 13:00–21:00 PDT, within 15 km of the fire. Rotation: half the velocity difference across an inbound–outbound couplet about 3 km wide (Vrot); 10, 15 and 20 m/s are the NWS weak, moderate and strong guide values [BW07]. Low-level outflow: divergence along the beam within 2 km of the ground of 0.0025 s−1 or more, the microburst criterion of Wilson et al. (1984) and Roberts & Wilson (1989) as used by Gibson & Carlin (2025) [W84; RW89; GC25]. Mid-level convergence: the same threshold 3–7 km up, the inflow that often precedes a downburst. Velocity differences are folded back into ± the Nyquist velocity; a signature must hold across a 3 × 3 patch of echo gates whose beam clears the ground by 300 m.

Limits

Beyond about 100 km the beam overshoots shallow outflow; no dealiasing; no storm tracking.

Sources
[BW07] · [W84] · [RW89] · [GC25] · [CA93]

Firefighter hazards: precursors and correlations Radar

What it shows

The polarimetric downburst precursors searched on every pyroCb and pyroCu day with a radar within 100 km, how often each appeared, and the rank correlations of the hazard signatures with the forecast factors.

How it's made

Gibson & Carlin's (2025) height-bin method on precipitation gates only: 95th-percentile reflectivity and KDP, and median ZDR and 10th-percentile ρhv in the reflectivity core, every scan for 90 minutes either side of the tallest plume [GC25]. The physics of the signatures follows Carlin & Ryzhkov (2025) and Kumjian (2013) [CR25; K13]. Correlations are Spearman's ρ [SP04].

Firefighter safety

The hazard chain Firefighter safety

What it shows

The sequence from dry fuel to a ground hazard under a pyroCb, and the indicator for each link.

Background

A convective column draws air in at its base and, when it collapses or its precipitation evaporates, drives air down and out: the indraft and downdraft hazards described for plume-dominated fires by Rothermel (1991) and in the extreme fire behaviour synthesis [R91; W11]. Evaporation-driven downdrafts and the outflow they produce are the microburst process documented with Doppler radar [W84; RW89].

Sources
[R91] · [W11] · [W84] · [RW89] · [TK21]

PyroCb triggers and DCAPE by class Firefighter safety

What it shows

Strip charts of FP/PFT, PCAPE and DCAPE for every fire day by plume class, with each one's best cut and held-out score.

How to read it

FP/PFT and PCAPE decide whether a pyroCb forms; DCAPE separates poorly as a pyroCb test, which is expected: it describes what the downdrafts can do once there is a storm, not whether there will be one. See strip charts.

Sources
[TK21] · DCAPE [GW98; E94] · HRRR [D22] · in depth: FP ÷ PFT, PCAPE, DCAPE

Which factor tracks which hazard Firefighter safety

What it shows

Spearman rank correlation of each radar hazard signature with each forecast factor on pyroCb and pyroCu days. Blue = a stronger signature with a higher factor value, red = with a lower value, grey = no relationship; a box marks p < 0.05.

How it's made

Signatures from the radar kinematics and polarimetric searches (kinematics, precursors); factors from HRRR (DCAPE, PCAPE, zfc, qML) and GOES (fire power). Low-level and core signatures use only radars within 100 km; convergence and rotation every range. Core ZDR is sign-flipped, since lower is stronger.

How to read it

Read across a row for the factor that best tracks that hazard. DCAPE's column is the one that lights up for the ground hazards; PCAPE and qML light up for the precipitation core instead. Shade bands are 0.15 wide.

Limits

Seven signatures × five factors = 35 tests: one or two could reach p < 0.05 by chance. Trust patterns that agree across rows (DCAPE with outflow, convergence and rotation) more than any single box.

Sources
[SP04] · [GC25] · [GW98] · [TK21] · palette: blue–red diverging with neutral grey midpoint

DCAPE and the outflow at the ground Firefighter safety

What it shows

Low-level outflow (radar divergence, 10−3 s−1) against the day's peak DCAPE, the share of days reaching the downburst threshold above and below DCAPE 1,235 J/kg, and the median outflow in each band.

How it's made

DCAPE: the energy a parcel from the lowest-θe air in the lowest 400 hPa gains descending moist-adiabatically to the ground, from HRRR soundings at the hot front, computed with MetPy [GW98; E94; M]. Outflow threshold 0.0025 s−1 [W84; GC25]. The 1,235 J/kg split is the best split on these days.

How to read it

Dots to the right of the vertical line and above the horizontal one are high-DCAPE days with downburst-strength outflow; they dominate. High DCAPE means dry air under the cloud base that the precipitation can chill by evaporation, so the downdraft accelerates all the way down.

Limits

25 days. DCAPE assumes a saturated downdraft. Two low-DCAPE outflow days came during strong offshore wind, which can make divergence of its own.

Sources
[GW98] · [E94] · [W84] · [GC25] · [M] · [D22]

DCAPE, inflow and rotation Firefighter safety

What it shows

Mid-level convergence and rotation (Vrot) against DCAPE, every radar range.

How to read it

Mid-level convergence draws dry environmental air into the core, where evaporation chills it; Gibson & Carlin found it before 83% of the downbursts they studied, making it the most consistent precursor [GC25]. Rotation values of 10 m/s and more are the NWS "weak" guide value [BW07].

[GC25] · [BW07] · [GW98]

The precipitation core doubles the outflow Firefighter safety

What it shows

Outflow and mid-level convergence on days with a precipitation core over the fire against days without, radar within 100 km; the bar is each group's median, the dashed line the 0.0025 s−1 threshold.

How it's made

A core is at least 10 precipitation gates (30 dBZ or more, ρhv ≥ 0.90, ZDR −2 to 8 dB) in a height bin over the fire in any scan [GC25].

How to read it

Without precipitation there is nothing to evaporate and little to load the downdraft, so the core is the hinge between "pyroCb" and "downburst". The water loading and melting that drive the downdraft are modelled in Carlin & Ryzhkov (2025) [CR25].

PCAPE and zfc set the core, not the outflow Firefighter safety

What it shows

Four small scatters: the strongest core (95th-percentile reflectivity) and the outflow, each against PCAPE and against the free-convection height zfc.

How to read it

More pyroconvective energy and a lower zfc go with heavier cores: a stronger, deeper updraft grows more precipitation. They do not go with the outflow at the ground, which depends on how dry the air under the core is (DCAPE). Compare the top pair with the bottom pair.

How often each precursor appeared Firefighter safety

What it shows

For pyroCb and pyroCu days with a radar within 100 km: the share with a precipitation core, a descending KDP core, a descending reflectivity core, a ZDR trough and a ρhv drop.

How to read it

A precursor is useful when the orange bar is long and the blue one short. The descending KDP core is the one that does that here. Gibson & Carlin found DRCs in 83%, descending KDP cores in 85% and ZDR troughs with ρhv drops in 89% of 53 U.S. downbursts [GC25].
