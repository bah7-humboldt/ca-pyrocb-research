Factor guide

The Factors page shows each ingredient of a pyroCb day in one line. This page goes deeper: what each factor measures, exactly how this study computes it, why the research says it should matter, where it breaks down, and the papers it comes from. The "In this study" box on each factor is filled from the study's own results.

Contents

Fire against the air

Fire power ÷ PFT

What it is. The fire's convective heat output in a given hour divided by the pyroCb firepower threshold (PFT) of the air above it in the same hour, taking the day's highest hourly value. PFT is the least fire power the atmosphere needs before a plume can become a pyroCb (see below), so the ratio says how close the fire came to what that day's air required.

How it's computed here. Fire power is GOES fire radiative power (FRP) inside the final perimeter plus about 3 km, averaged per hour and multiplied by 3.9 to turn radiated into convective heat: radiation is only a small share of the energy a fire releases (Wooster et al. 2005), and Tory & Kepert (2021, appendix D) use the same kind of conversion. PFT comes from the HRRR analysis at the day's hot front for the same hour, 12:00–22:00 PDT.

Why it matters. Tory & Kepert (2021) built PFT so that a pyroCb is expected once fire power reaches it, with the ratio at 1. Their framework treats the plume as an entraining thermal (Morton et al. 1956) that has to reach the height where its added heat and moisture make it freely convective (Tory et al. 2018). Neither the fire nor the atmosphere alone decides the outcome; the ratio puts both in one number. That's why it is the best separator here, north and south.

Limits. The study's cut sits near 0.25, not 1: GOES FRP is an hourly snapshot of a fire partly hidden by smoke and pixel saturation, the ×3.9 conversion is rough, and PFT assumes an efficient, undiluted plume. Treat 0.25 as this pipeline's calibration, not a physical constant.

Sources: Tory & Kepert 2021 [TK21]; Tory et al. 2018 [TTK18]; Morton et al. 1956 [MTT56]; Wooster et al. 2005 [W05].

Pyrocumulonimbus firepower threshold (PFT)

What it is. The least fire power, in gigawatts, that the atmosphere needs before a plume can rise to the height where it convects freely and grows into a pyroCb. Lower PFT means air more ready for a pyroCb. "No solution" means no fire power would do it: the air is too dry or too stable all the way to the −20 °C level.

How it's computed here. The full procedure of Tory & Kepert (2021, section 4), not their shortcut:

  1. A mixed layer is averaged from the ground up, weighting each height by its distance above the ground (the plume's entrainment grows with height), and deepened until its condensation level falls inside it.
  2. From it, the saturation-point (SP) curve: the cloud bases a plume would reach as the fire adds more heat, with about 1 g/kg of moisture for every 15 K of warmth from combustion.
  3. The free-convection point zfc is the lowest point on that curve from which a parcel stays warmer than the air (by a 1 K buffer) all the way to −20 °C.
  4. PFT = π cp (b′/(1 + a′b′))² ρ0 zfc² UML Δθfc, the paper's equation 25.

The study's code reproduces the paper's Chisholm case (459 vs 520 GW). The day's value is the lowest hourly PFT, 12:00–22:00 PDT.

Why it matters. It is the atmospheric half of the problem in one number. On its own it misses fires that are too small for very receptive air, and it can't tell a quiet day from a pyroCb day in the same air mass.

Limits. PFT is sensitive to boundary-layer moisture and depth, where HRRR has known biases (Dowell et al. 2022). Small moisture errors move zfc, and PFT goes with zfc², so they matter twice.

Sources: [TK21]; [TTK18]; Dowell et al. 2022 [D22].

Fire power, peak hour

What it is. The fire's peak hourly convective heat release, in GW, from GOES-West (GOES-16 for the 2018 fires).

How it's computed here. The GOES fire detection product's FRP for every fire pixel inside the perimeter, summed per scan, averaged per hour and multiplied by 3.9. Hours missing from the primary satellite's archive are filled from GOES-16, scaled by 1.5 to match the primary satellite's view (the ratio found where both saw Dixie).

Why it matters. FRP measures how fast fuel is burning (Wooster et al. 2005): a bigger, hotter fire makes a stronger plume. But a pyroCb needs the right air too, so fire power alone separates poorly. Many of the study's biggest runs were wind-driven with no deep plume cloud.

Limits. Geostationary FRP misses fire under thick smoke or cloud and saturates over intense fire, so it undercounts the most extreme hours. Pixels are about 2 km under the satellite and larger at California's slanted view (Schmidt 2020).

Sources: [W05]; Schmidt 2020 [S20]; [TK21] appendix D.

The atmosphere's ingredients

Pyroconvective CAPE (PCAPE)

What it is. The buoyant energy of the pyroCb parcel above its free-convection point: the area between the parcel's path and the environment's temperature, from zfc to where the parcel stops being buoyant (its equilibrium level), in J/kg. Zero when the day has no pyroCb parcel at all.

How it's computed here. Rd∫(Tv,parcel − Tv,env) d ln p from zfc to the equilibrium level, using the same parcel as PFT and the HRRR column up to 100 hPa. If the parcel is still buoyant at 100 hPa, the value is a lower bound. The day's value is the largest hourly PCAPE.

Why it matters. It is ordinary CAPE (Moncrieff & Miller 1976; Emanuel 1994) applied to a fire-heated parcel instead of a surface one. PFT says how hard it is to start a pyroCb; PCAPE says how vigorously it could grow once started. It is not in Tory & Kepert's paper; this study added it, and it is the best of the atmosphere-only measures.

Limits. Like any parcel CAPE it ignores entrainment above zfc and condensate weight, so it overstates the energy a real plume gets.

Sources: Moncrieff & Miller 1976 [MM76]; Emanuel 1994 [E94]; [TK21].

Downdraft CAPE (DCAPE)

What it is. The energy a parcel of mid-level air gains as it sinks to the ground, kept saturated by rain or ice evaporating into it, in J/kg. It measures how strong a downdraft and its gusty outflow at the surface could be.

How it's computed here. MetPy's downdraft_cape on each hourly HRRR column. The parcel starts at the lowest equivalent potential temperature in the lowest 400 hPa (the driest air) and descends along its wet-bulb pseudo-adiabat to the surface; DCAPE is the area between that path and the environment. The day's value is the largest from 12:00 to 22:00 PDT. Every sounding draws the parcel as a green dash-dot line.

Why it matters. High DCAPE marks dry mid-levels over a hot, deep, dry surface layer (Gilmore & Wicker 1998), the setup for strong downdrafts and erratic outflow winds when a convective column collapses. For fire crews that is the danger of a plume-dominated fire (Rothermel 1991). Here DCAPE runs higher on pyroCb days, but as a pyroCb test it is weak: it describes the hazard once convection exists, not whether it forms. For crew safety it is the factor that matters: on pyroCb and pyroCu days it is the only forecast factor that goes with stronger radar outflow (ρ +0.43), mid-level convergence (+0.41) and rotation (+0.31). With DCAPE above about 1,235 J/kg, outflow reached the downburst threshold on 10 of 13 days, against 5 of 12 below it. See Firefighter safety.

Limits. DCAPE is a ceiling: it assumes enough rain or ice to keep the parcel saturated all the way down, which a high-based, dry pyroCb may not have.

Sources: Gilmore & Wicker 1998 [GW98]; Rothermel 1991 [R91]; MetPy [M].

Free-convection height zfc

What it is. The height above the ground where a fire-heated, moistened parcel becomes freely buoyant all the way to −20 °C: the point on the saturation-point curve that the plume has to reach (the star on every sounding).

How it's computed here. As step 3 of PFT above, at the hour of the day's lowest PFT, in km above ground.

Why it matters. A plume dilutes as it rises, entraining the air around it (Morton et al. 1956), so the higher it has to go, the more heat it needs: PFT grows with zfc². A low zfc comes with a moist mixed layer and moisture aloft (Lareau & Clements 2016; Tory et al. 2018).

Limits. Defined only when a solution exists; on "no solution" days it is missing, which hides how far from a solution the air was.

Sources: [TK21]; [TTK18]; [MTT56]; Lareau & Clements 2016 [LC16].

Extra plume warmth Δθfc

What it is. How much warmer than the surrounding mixed layer (in potential temperature, K) the plume must be to reach zfc and convect.

How it's computed here. θSP − θML at the free-convection point, at the hour of lowest PFT.

Why it matters. PFT is directly proportional to it. Warm, stable layers above the mixed layer force a larger Δθfc; a deep, well-mixed afternoon layer with a cool, moist layer aloft keeps it small (Tory & Kepert 2021).

Limits. Very sensitive to the temperature profile just above the mixed layer, where a 3 km model blurs inversions.

Sources: [TK21]; [TTK18].

Mixed-layer wind UML

What it is. The average wind from the ground up to zfc, in m/s.

How it's computed here. The magnitude of the height-averaged wind vector over that layer, at the hour of lowest PFT.

Why it matters. Wind bends a plume over and mixes in more surrounding air, so a stronger wind demands more fire power: PFT is proportional to UML (Tory & Kepert 2021). But wind also drives fast spread and so raises fire power, which is why wind alone points both ways. The study's biggest wind-driven runs had no pyroCb.

Limits. One average speed hides shear, which can tilt or tear a plume apart.

Sources: [TK21]; [LC16].

Mixed-layer moisture qML

What it is. The specific humidity of the mixed layer the fire's heat goes into, in g/kg; the day's highest hourly value.

How it's computed here. The same height-weighted mixed-layer average PFT uses, from the HRRR analysis at the hot front.

Why it matters. Moisture sets the plume's cloud base: a moister mixed layer condenses lower, so the plume reaches free convection with less heat. Combustion adds some water, but the environment's moisture usually matters more (Potter 2005; Luderer et al. 2006). The study's no-plume days had very dry mixed layers (median about 1 g/kg against 4 on pyroCb days).

Limits. Model boundary-layer moisture is one of HRRR's weaker fields over complex terrain.

Sources: Potter 2005 [P05]; Luderer et al. 2006 [L06]; [TK21]; [D22].

700 hPa moisture (ERA5)

What it is. Specific humidity about 3 km up (700 hPa), in g/kg, from the ERA5 reanalysis at the day's hot front.

How it's computed here. ERA5 (Hersbach et al. 2020), the moister of the 18Z and 00Z analyses. The same field is forecast days ahead by the global and AI weather ensembles, which is why it was chosen.

Why it matters. California pyroCbs mostly came with mid-level moisture moving north under a hot ridge, the same pattern that brings high-based, dry thunderstorms (Rorig & Ferguson 1999). Moist air above the mixed layer lowers zfc and lets the column grow once it convects (Lareau & Clements 2016). On the Weather patterns page it combines with the weather regime as a days-ahead "pattern allows a pyroCb" signal.

Limits. ERA5 is a ~25 km grid; its moisture is a broad-scale view, not the column over the fire.

Sources: Hersbach et al. 2020 [H20]; Rorig & Ferguson 1999 [RF99]; [LC16].

Fuels and terrain

Fuel ahead: available fuel

What it is. The fuel that could burn in a flaming front (kg/m²) on the unburned ground within 5 km of the previous day's growth: where the fire was heading.

How it's computed here. LANDFIRE (Rollins 2009) from before the fire: the newest fuel vintage before the fire year, with every later disturbance layer up to the year before the fire. Each cell's Scott & Burgan (2005) fuel model gives its 1-h, 10-h, live herb and live woody loads, plus canopy fuel where the stand can carry a crown fire (see the next factor).

Why it matters. Fuel is the energy available to the plume. It is prognostic: it is known the morning before.

Limits. It barely differs between pyroCb and no-plume days. These are timber fires throughout, so fuel varies much less from day to day than the atmosphere does. Fuel-model loads are representative values, not measurements.

Sources: Rollins 2009 [R09]; Scott & Burgan 2005 [SB05].

Fuel ahead: canopy fuel load

What it is. The crown fuel in the stand ahead of the fire (kg/m²).

How it's computed here. LANDFIRE canopy bulk density × (canopy height − canopy base height). A cell counts as able to crown when canopy cover is at least 40%, the canopy base is 2 m or lower and canopy bulk density is at least 0.10 kg/m³. These are the study's own cut-offs, based on the crown-fire ideas of Scott & Reinhardt (2001).

Why it matters. A crown fire releases far more energy per area than a surface fire, and crown runs feed the largest plumes (Rothermel 1991).

Limits. LANDFIRE canopy layers are modelled from satellite and plot data and carry large cell-level error.

Sources: Scott & Reinhardt 2001 [SR01]; [R09]; [R91].

Fuel ahead: slope

What it is. The mean slope (degrees) of the unburned ground within 5 km of the previous day's growth.

How it's computed here. LANDFIRE 2020 terrain over the same cells as the fuel-ahead factors.

Why it matters. Fire spreads faster upslope (Rothermel 1972), so steep ground ahead can raise the day's fire power. Terrain also channels winds.

Limits. Mean slope says nothing about aspect or whether the fire is running up or down it.

Sources: Rothermel 1972 [R72]; [R09].

Burned that day: available fuel

What it is. The same available-fuel measure as above, but on the ground that actually burned that day.

How it's computed here. The satellite burn-day map (each 100 m cell dated by its first fire detection) overlaid on the pre-fire LANDFIRE fuel.

Why it matters. It is the fuel load behind the fuel-based fire power, but known only after the day.

Limits. As for fuel ahead.

Sources: [SB05]; [R09].

Both start from the same pre-fire LANDFIRE map and the same burned area and burning time; they differ in how much fuel they count and whether moisture decides how much of it burns. Both are kept so analysts can compare them.

LANDFIRE fuel fire power (Scott & Burgan)

What it is. Fire power estimated from the fuel side instead of the satellite: how much energy the day's burned area released and over how long, in GW, using the fire behavior fuel models.

How it's computed here. 0.7 × 15 MJ/kg × available fuel × area burned ÷ burning time (Tory & Kepert 2021, appendix D):

  • 15 MJ/kg is the heat a kilogram of fuel releases.
  • 0.7 is the share that goes into the plume as convection.
  • Available fuel is the Scott & Burgan (2005) 1-h, 10-h, live herb and live woody loads plus canopy fuel where the stand can crown.
  • Burning time is the day's total GOES fire energy divided by its peak hourly FRP, at least 3 hours.

The idea is Byram's fireline intensity (1959) applied to a whole day's area.

Fuel moisture: assumed, not used. All of the available fuel is assumed to burn, and the 15 MJ/kg heat yield stands for dry fuel. That is reasonable on the studied days, which were all very dry, but it would overstate fire power in moister conditions, when part of the fine fuel and the green shrubs would not burn or would give up heat boiling off water.

Why it matters. It can be forecast (forecast spread area × fuel ÷ forecast duration), unlike satellite fire power. It is also an independent check on GOES, which misses fire under smoke. It confirmed GOES on the Creek Fire, 5 September.

Limits. Leaves out duff and large wood (see the comparison); fuel-model loads are representative values, not measurements.

Sources: [TK21]; Byram 1959 [B59]; [SB05]; [W05].

LANDFIRE fuel fire power ÷ PFT

What it is. The fuel fire power above divided by the day's lowest PFT: the fire-against-air ratio with fuel standing in for the satellite.

How it's computed here. As above, against the day's lowest HRRR PFT.

Why it matters. It is the forecastable version of FP/PFT. The Forecasting page tests it with the previous day's area and the morning HRRR forecast.

Limits. Carries the errors of both the fuel estimate and PFT; the FCCS version below scores better.

Sources: [TK21]; [B59].

FCCS fuel fire power (FOFEM, surface fuel)

What it is. The same fuel-side fire power, but with the fuel that FOFEM says actually burns, from the LANDFIRE Fuel Characteristic Classification System (FCCS) fuelbeds.

How it's computed here.

  1. Fuelbeds. FCCS (Ottmar et al. 2007; Riccardi et al. 2007) describes each fuelbed by layer: canopy, shrubs, herbs, dead wood by size, litter and duff. LANDFIRE maps them: LF2016 for fires through 2023, LF2023 for 2024.
  2. Consumption. The toolkit's copy of SpatialFOFEM, the First Order Fire Effects Model (Reinhardt et al. 1997; Lutes 2020), runs once per season on a table of every fuelbed × 10-h moisture (3–16%) × 1000-h moisture (3–30%), for the Pacific West region.
    • Litter and dead wood consumption comes from the Burnup model (Albini & Reinhardt 1995).
    • Duff consumption comes from duff moisture, set to 3 × 1000-h.
    • Herbs and shrubs follow FOFEM's rules.
  3. Each day. The burned cells take their row of that table at the day's gridMET moisture (Abatzoglou 2013), with 10-h = 100-h − 2.
  4. Fire power. As for the LANDFIRE version: 0.7 × 15 MJ/kg × consumed surface fuel × area ÷ burning time. Crown fuel is left out of this version (FCCS foliage loads are heavy and don't depend on moisture).

Fuel moisture: integrated. Moisture decides how much duff and wood burns. On the studied days it changed little: all were dry, and holding moisture fixed gives the same score. Live fuel moisture is not part of FOFEM's consumption (see the comparison).

Why it matters. Counting the duff, litter and heavy wood that a long timber run consumes makes the fuel side agree with the satellite much better than the Scott & Burgan version does.

Limits. FCCS loadings are mapped from vegetation type, not measured; duff and logs partly smoulder after the front, so not all of this energy reaches the plume in the same hour. Calculations "provided with the command line applications developed by the Missoula Fire Sciences Laboratory, Missoula, MT".

Sources: [O07]; [RI07]; [R97]; [L20]; [AR95]; [A13]; [TK21].

FCCS fuel fire power ÷ PFT

What it is. The FCCS fuel fire power divided by the day's lowest PFT.

How it's computed here. As above, against the day's lowest HRRR PFT.

Why it matters. It is the best fuel-based separator in the study, as good as satellite fire power ÷ PFT, and it can be forecast once a spread area is forecast.

Limits. As for the FCCS fire power and for PFT.

Sources: [TK21]; [R97]; [O07].

Fuel moisture

The fuel moisture baseline for a pyroCb

Why a baseline, not a threshold. Every day in this study is a big fire day (at least 1,000 acres of growth or 3 GW of fire power), and all were dry, so moisture hardly separates pyroCb days from the rest. It works as a precondition: pyroCbs only happened when fuels were at least this dry. The table gives, for each measure, the range on the 13 pyroCb days, the wettest pyroCb day (the edge of the envelope: no pyroCb occurred in moister fuels), how many other big fire days were just as dry, and how much of each PSA's full-year record (March 2005 to 2025, the same record the fire danger charts rate percentiles against) is that dry.

Read the edge as "no pyroCb in this study happened in fuels wetter than this", not as a trigger: most days that dry made no pyroCb, because the air (PFT) and the fire's power decide. Thirteen pyroCb days is a small sample, and the edge is set by a single day.

Sources: Bradshaw et al. 1984 [B84]; Jolly et al. 2024 [J24]; [A13]; National Fuel Moisture Database via FEMS [NFMD].

Energy Release Component (PSA)

What it is. The NFDRS Energy Release Component: the energy per unit area available at the flaming front, which rises as dead and live fuels dry over days to weeks and changes little with daily weather (Bradshaw et al. 1984).

How it's computed here. The ERC for NFDRS 2016 fuel model Y (Jolly et al. 2024), averaged over the RAWS of the fire's Predictive Services Area: the same station groups and records behind the ONCC and OSCC fire danger charts.

Why it matters. It is the standard measure of seasonal dryness that analysts already watch, and large-fire activity rises with it.

Limits. A PSA average over many stations, some far from the fire; it barely separates pyroCb days from other big fire days.

Sources: [B84]; [J24].

ERC percentile

What it is. Where the day's PSA ERC ranks among every day of the same PSA's record, March 2005 to 2025, all year round (100 = highest on record).

How it's computed here. Empirical rank against that PSA's full-year record, the same basis (period, every day of the year, PSA = per-date mean of its stations) as the percentiles on the ONCC/OSCC fire danger charts and PSA danger maps, so the numbers match what analysts already watch, and a coastal and an eastside fire can be compared.

Why it matters. Percentiles are how fire danger is briefed (90th / 97th). Every pyroCb day here was at or above the 86th percentile of its PSA's full-year record, half of them above the 96th.

Limits. The record starts in 2005; percentiles of a short record move as years are added.

Sources: [B84]; [J24].

1000-h fuel moisture (PSA)

What it is. NFDRS moisture of 3–8 inch dead wood, which follows weeks of weather: the slow-drying heavy fuel.

How it's computed here. PSA average of the station NFDRS 1000-h values (fuel model Y).

Why it matters. Heavy dead fuel and duff only burn completely when this is low; it sets how much of a timber fire's fuel can feed the fire (Reinhardt et al. 1997).

Limits. As for ERC.

Sources: [B84]; [J24]; [R97].

100-h fuel moisture (PSA)

What it is. NFDRS moisture of 1–3 inch dead wood, responding over a few days.

How it's computed here. PSA average of the station values.

Why it matters. It tracks the recent drying that lets mid-size fuels join the flaming front.

Limits. As for ERC.

Sources: [B84]; [J24].

1000-h fuel moisture at the fire (gridMET)

What it is. The 1000-hour dead fuel moisture on gridMET's 4 km grid at the fire's hot front, computed from the gridded weather with the NFDRS equations (Abatzoglou 2013).

How it's computed here. The day's value at the cell nearest the hot front. It is the moisture the FCCS/FOFEM fire power uses for duff and log consumption.

Why it matters. It is local to the fire, unlike the PSA average, and continuous in space and time.

Limits. Modelled from gridded weather rather than measured; on the studied days it stayed within 3.8–9.7%.

Sources: [A13]; [B84].

Shrub live fuel moisture (field samples)

What it is. Measured moisture of live shrub foliage, as a percentage of dry weight.

How it's computed here. Field samples from the National Fuel Moisture Database (through FEMS) at every site within 60 km of the fire, the latest sample per site and species in the 21 days before, averaged over sites. Values outside 30–400% are dropped as errors (the database holds "live" conifer samples at 5–10%).

Why it matters. Green fuel burns less readily and gives up heat boiling off its water, about 2.6 MJ per kg of water.

Limits. Samples are every two weeks or so, at a handful of sites, mostly chamise and manzanita; they may not represent the fuel the fire is burning. It shows no skill here, and a live-moisture heat penalty changed the fuel fire power only about 4%.

Sources: [NFMD]; [J24].

What was observed

Radar: fire-rooted plume echo top

What it is. The top of the radar echo column rising from the fire's ash and debris, in km above sea level; the day's second-highest scan.

How it's computed here. NEXRAD dual-polarization scans every ~20 minutes. Echo with a correlation coefficient below 0.80 is debris, not weather. That boundary is from Kumjian (2013); fire plumes are mostly below 0.4 (Melnikov et al. 2008). A column counts only if its lowest echo sits on the past hour's GOES fire pixels and it climbs without big gaps. Radars badly cluttered by terrain are left out.

Why it matters. Radar sees the plume column directly, through smoke and cloud. A debris column capped by ice (correlation 0.95 or more above 6 km) is the pyroCb signature seen from aircraft and radar together (Peterson et al. 2022). A 10.8 km top separates pyroCb days almost perfectly here, but only on usable radars, and only after the fact. See the Radar page.

Limits. Range and terrain: far from the radar the beam is wide and high. It is an observation, not a forecast.

Sources: Kumjian 2013 [K13]; Melnikov et al. 2008 [M08]; Peterson et al. 2022 [P22]; Lareau et al. 2022 [L22].

OMPS aerosol index, same-day pass

What it is. The peak UV Aerosol Index within 150 km of the fire front on the satellite's ~13:30 local pass.

How it's computed here. The 0–50 "pyrocumulonimbus" aerosol index layer that NASA FIRMS shows, read from NASA GIBS. For these fires it is Suomi NPP OMPS, with 50 km pixels.

Why it matters. The index is high where UV-absorbing smoke sits above the scattering atmosphere below, and it grows with the smoke's height (Torres et al. 1998). Smoke a pyroCb lifts into the upper troposphere or stratosphere reads far above ordinary smoke (Fromm et al. 2010; Peterson et al. 2018).

Limits. At 50 km a single-cell California pyroCb reads like an ordinary smoky day, and regional smoke (September 2020) pushes no-plume days high. Most pyroCbs form after the 13:30 pass. See the Satellite page.

Sources: Torres et al. 1998 [T98]; Fromm et al. 2010 [F10]; Peterson et al. 2018 [P18].

OMPS aerosol index, next-day pass

What it is. The peak aerosol index within 500 km of the fire on the next day's pass, when smoke from an afternoon pyroCb would first be seen, downwind.

How it's computed here. As for the same-day pass, one day later and over a wider circle.

Why it matters. It catches lofted smoke the same-day pass came too early for.

Limits. A 500 km circle takes in other fires' smoke; it shows no skill here.

Sources: [T98]; [F10].

Satellite growth

What it is. Acres burned that fire day (06:00 to 06:00 local).

How it's computed here. The toolkit's satellite growth set:

  • VIIRS 375 m and MODIS active-fire detections from NASA FIRMS (Schroeder et al. 2014; Giglio et al. 2016), inside the fire's MTBS burned-area perimeter (Eidenshink et al. 2007);
  • each 100 m cell dated by its first detection;
  • for Dixie, a total within 0.1% of MTBS's (381,417 against 381,529 acres).

Why it matters. Big runs and pyroCbs often go together, but the study found that most growth happened on days with no pyroCb solution at all: wind-driven, not plume-driven.

Limits. Detections come a few times a day, so very fast runs between passes are dated late. It describes the day after the fact.
